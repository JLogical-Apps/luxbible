# The Coming of Christ: A 27-Day Bible Study


## Days 1–9: The promise before Bethlehem

These days establish why Christ’s coming was needed, what God had promised, and the varied lives through which the biblical story moved toward His birth.

| Day | Character and perspective | Biblical readings | Study focus and central question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Eve — Hope in the aftermath of the fall | Genesis 3:1–21; Hebrews 2:14–18 | Begin with humanity’s need for deliverance. Read the promise concerning the woman’s offspring in light of Christ’s victory over the devil. What hope does God give amid judgment, and why does our Deliverer share our humanity? |
| 2 | Abraham — A promise reaching the nations | Genesis 12:1–9; Galatians 3:7–16; John 8:56–58 | Trace the relationship between God’s promise to Abraham, the promised offspring, and blessing for the nations. Jesus explicitly connects Abraham’s joy with His own day. How does Abraham’s story prepare us to understand the worldwide significance of Christ’s coming? |
| 3 | Tamar — A vulnerable woman within the promised line | Genesis 38; Matthew 1:1–3 | Attend to Tamar’s vulnerability, Judah’s failures, and his acknowledgment in Genesis 38:26. Matthew includes this family history in Jesus’ genealogy. What does this account reveal about the human history through which God brought the Messiah? |
| 4 | Rahab — An outsider who trusts Israel’s God | Joshua 2:1–21; 6:22–25; Matthew 1:5; Hebrews 11:31 | Study Rahab’s confession about God and the actions that express her faith. Then notice her place in the Messiah’s ancestry. What did Rahab come to believe about God, and how did that belief change her allegiance and future? |
| 5 | Ruth — Finding refuge and becoming part of the story | Ruth 1:6–18; 2:10–12; 4:13–22; Matthew 1:5–6 | Follow the movement from loss and displacement to refuge, belonging, and the family line of David. How does Ruth’s personal story become part of something much larger than she could see? |
| 6 | David — Receiving the promise of an enduring kingdom | 2 Samuel 7:8–29; Luke 1:31–33 | Read both God’s promise and David’s prayerful response. Then compare Gabriel’s announcement about Jesus’ throne and kingdom. What kind of King does the promise to David lead us to expect? |
| 7 | Isaiah — Announcing light and the promised child | Isaiah 7:10–16; 9:1–7; Matthew 1:18–23 | Read Isaiah in his original setting before tracing Matthew’s identification of Jesus as Immanuel. What do these passages reveal about the child’s identity and the hope His coming brings? |
| 8 | Micah — Expecting a ruler from Bethlehem | Micah 5:1–5; Matthew 2:1–6 | Examine the contrast between Bethlehem’s apparent smallness and the significance of its promised ruler. How will this ruler govern, and what does His shepherding reveal about the character of His kingdom? |
| 9 | Malachi — Calling people to prepare for the Lord | Malachi 3:1–4; 4:1–6; Luke 1:13–17 | Connect the promised messenger with the announcement of John’s ministry. Notice that the Lord’s coming involves purification and changed hearts. What does genuine preparation for the Lord require? |


For these early days, I would consistently distinguish what the person knew then from what later Scripture reveals about their place in Christ’s coming. That distinction is especially useful for Tamar, Rahab, and Ruth.

## Days 10–15: Receiving the announcement

These studies bring readers into the households and lives directly affected by the announcements surrounding Jesus’ birth.

| Day | Character and perspective | Biblical readings | Study focus and central question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | Zechariah — From difficulty believing to prophetic praise | Luke 1:5–25, 57–80 | Compare Zechariah’s initial response with the praise he offers when his speech returns. Notice how his song reaches beyond his own son to God’s covenant and salvation. How does his understanding grow from a personal answered prayer into a vision of God’s larger work? |
| 11 | Elizabeth — Recognizing the Lord’s arrival | Luke 1:24–25, 39–45, 57–66 | Study Elizabeth’s gratitude, her Spirit-enabled recognition of Mary’s child, and her affirmation of Mary’s faith. What does Elizabeth recognize about Jesus before His birth, and how does that recognition shape her words to Mary? |
| 12 | Gabriel — Delivering God’s announcement | Luke 1:11–20, 26–38 | Compare the two announcements Gabriel delivers. Focus on the names, identities, and missions assigned to John and Jesus. What does God want the recipients to understand about the child Mary will bear? |
| 13 | Mary — Believing, praising, and pondering | Luke 1:26–56; 2:1–19 | Trace Mary’s question, her response of faith, her song, and her later pondering. Notice how her praise draws on God’s mercy and promises to Israel. How does Mary receive God’s word while continuing to reflect on events she has not fully understood? |
| 14 | Joseph — Obedience expressed through action | Matthew 1:18–25; 2:13–23 | Observe what Joseph does after each divine instruction: he receives Mary, names Jesus, and protects the child. What does trusting God require of Joseph when obedience changes his plans and responsibilities? |
| 15 | John the Baptist — A life that points to Another | Luke 1:13–17, 39–45, 76–80; John 1:19–34 | Connect the purpose announced before John’s birth with his later testimony about Jesus. How does John understand his own identity in relation to Christ, and what does he want others to recognize about Jesus? |


John’s adult testimony is included deliberately: it lets readers see how the purpose announced around his birth develops into his ministry.

## Days 16–21: Witnessing and responding

These days place different responses alongside one another: proclamation, patient recognition, thanksgiving, scriptural knowledge, opposition, and worship.

| Day | Character and perspective | Biblical readings | Study focus and central question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 16 | The shepherds — Hearing, seeing, and telling | Luke 2:1–20 | Follow the sequence of announcement, journey, discovery, testimony, and praise. This reading also includes the heavenly host’s proclamation. What do the shepherds learn about Jesus, and how does their response develop as they act on what they have heard? |
| 17 | Simeon — Recognizing salvation after waiting | Luke 2:22–35 | Examine what Simeon has been awaiting and what he says upon seeing Jesus. Hold together salvation, light for the nations, glory for Israel, and coming opposition. How does Simeon’s blessing reveal both the hope and the cost associated with Christ’s coming? |
| 18 | Anna — Turning long faithfulness into thanksgiving and witness | Luke 2:22–38; Isaiah 52:7–10 | Read Anna’s brief appearance within the full temple scene. Notice her worship, prayer, thanksgiving, and speech to others awaiting redemption. How does her life of waiting become a life of testimony when Jesus arrives? |
| 19 | The chief priests and scribes — Knowing where to look | Matthew 2:1–12; Micah 5:2–5 | Study their accurate answer about the Messiah’s birthplace alongside the actions of the other people in the passage. Matthew records their answer but does not describe their subsequent response. What response should knowledge of the promised Messiah produce? |
| 20 | Herod — Resisting the arrival of the King | Matthew 2:1–18; Psalm 2 | Trace Herod’s alarm, deception, and violence. Attend also to the suffering his actions cause. What does Herod’s response expose about the conflict between preserving our own rule and receiving God’s King? |
| 21 | The Magi — Seeking and honoring the King | Matthew 2:1–12; Psalm 72:8–11 | Examine their journey, joy, worship, gifts, and response to God’s warning. Use Psalm 72 as the wider biblical picture of the nations honoring the king. What does their response reveal about the worth of Jesus and the reach of His coming? |


I would keep the Magi as Day 21 because their worship makes a fitting transition into six days devoted more directly to God’s purposes in the incarnation.

The order here is thematic. Simeon and Anna encounter Jesus at the temple after His birth, and Matthew places the Magi’s visit at a house. These should remain distinct scenes as you develop the studies (Luke 2:22–38; Matthew 2:11).

## Days 22–23: God the Father


### Day 22 — The Father sends the Son to bring us into His family

Read: Galatians 4:1–7; Ephesians 1:3–14.

Study focus: The Father’s purpose, the appointed time of Christ’s coming, redemption, and adoption. Notice how the sending of the Son and the sending of the Spirit belong together.

Questions:
- What do these passages reveal about why the Father sent His Son?
- How are Christ’s birth, His redeeming work, and our adoption connected?
- How does being received as God’s children deepen our understanding of Christmas?

### Day 23 — The Father reveals His love through the gift of His Son

Read: 1 John 4:7–14; John 3:16–21.

Study focus: God’s love expressed in sending the Son so that we might live through Him. Follow the passages from God’s initiative to the responses they call for.

Questions:
- How do these passages define God’s love through what He does?
- What do they say the Son was sent to accomplish?
- How should receiving this love shape our belief, conduct, and love for others?

## Days 24–25: Jesus Christ, God the Son


### Day 24 — The eternal Word becomes flesh

Read: John 1:1–18; Philippians 2:5–11.
Additional reading: Hebrews 1:1–4.

Study focus: Who Jesus is before His birth, His true humanity, and His willing humility. Bethlehem is the beginning of His human life, while John identifies Him as the eternal Word.

Questions:
- What do these passages affirm about Jesus’ identity before His birth?
- What does He take upon Himself in becoming human?
- How do His glory and humility belong together, and what response do they call for?

### Day 25 — The Son comes to do the Father’s will and save His people

Read: Hebrews 10:1–10; 2:14–18.

Study focus: Christ’s willing obedience, His sharing in our humanity, and the purpose of His self-offering.

This is especially well suited to your desire to study Christ’s own perspective: Hebrews 10 explicitly presents His coming into the world in terms of doing God’s will. It gives you a scriptural basis for discussing His purpose in coming. Bible Gateway

Questions:
- What does Hebrews say Christ came to do?
- Why is His sharing in our humanity essential to His saving work?
- How does connecting the manger with His obedience and sacrifice deepen our understanding of His birth?

## Days 26–27: God the Holy Spirit


### Day 26 — The Spirit brings about the conception of the holy Son

Read: Luke 1:26–38; Matthew 1:18–25.

Study focus: The Spirit’s work in Jesus’ conception and what the announcements reveal about Jesus’ identity.

Questions:
- What do Luke and Matthew explicitly attribute to the Holy Spirit?
- How do the announcements connect the child’s conception with His identity and mission?
- What does this reveal about God’s initiative in accomplishing what humanity could not bring about?
This day should keep the distinction clear: the Son becomes incarnate; the Father sends Him; the Spirit brings about His miraculous conception.

### Day 27 — The Spirit enables recognition, praise, and witness

Read: Luke 1:39–45, 67–79; 2:25–35.
Additional reading: John 15:26.

Study focus: Revisit Elizabeth, Zechariah, and Simeon with particular attention to the Spirit’s activity. John 15:26 supplies later teaching about the Spirit’s testimony to Christ.

Questions:
- What does the Spirit enable these people to recognize and proclaim?
- What themes recur in their testimony about Jesus?
- After these 27 days, what can we now confess, praise, and tell others about Christ?
This final day brings the plan back through earlier encounters and helps readers see God’s work within them.
