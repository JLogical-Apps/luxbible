# Lux Memory Research

This is a working bibliography for the learning science, Scripture-memory practices, market observations, and policy constraints that may shape Lux Memory. It supports the exploratory ideas in [roadmap.md](roadmap.md) and [technical.md](technical.md). It does not make those ideas settled requirements.

Last reviewed: August 2, 2026

## How to read the sources

- **Learning evidence** includes experiments and reviews. It is the strongest basis for the practice loop, but most studies did not test exact, passage-length Scripture recall.
- **Field practice** records methods developed by Scripture-memory ministries. These methods have practical history, but should not be described as experimentally proven unless a study supports them.
- **User experience** includes product documentation and public reviews. These sources reveal needs and frustrations, but are self-selected and anecdotal.
- **Policy and legal references** identify constraints worth planning around, not legal advice. Store policies and laws change, so recheck the current primary source and consult qualified counsel when needed before implementation or launch.

## Research-backed learning principles

### Retrieval should be the center of practice

Trying to produce an answer from memory generally creates stronger long-term retention than repeatedly studying the answer. Activities such as typing, reciting, or reconstructing a passage should therefore carry more evidence of memory than reading, highlighting, or choosing from visible options.

- [Roediger and Karpicke, Test-Enhanced Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x): repeated testing improved delayed retention more than repeated study, even when study looked better on an immediate test.
- [Roediger and Butler, The Critical Role of Retrieval Practice in Long-Term Retention](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20951630/): reviews evidence for retrieval practice and explains the importance of feedback.
- [Dunlosky et al., Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/): rates practice testing and distributed practice among the most broadly useful learning techniques reviewed.
- [Rawson and Zamary, Why Is Free Recall Practice More Effective Than Recognition Practice?](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749596X19300026): supports using production from memory, rather than recognition alone, when later recall is the goal.

**Implication for Lux Memory:** recognition and visible-word activities can teach or scaffold a passage, but an unassisted production task should be required before the app presents it as reliably memorized.

### Feedback should be immediate enough to correct errors

Retrieval is not enough when a learner confidently recalls the wrong wording. The app should show the correct text after an attempt and make omissions, substitutions, and ordering errors easy to understand.

- [Butler, Karpicke, and Roediger, Correcting a Metacognitive Error](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18605878/): feedback following retrieval was especially useful for correcting errors, including errors made with low confidence.
- [Roediger and Butler review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20951630/): summarizes evidence that feedback increases the benefits and reliability of retrieval practice.

**Implication for Lux Memory:** scoring should lead to correction and another opportunity to recall. It should not merely award points or mark an attempt wrong.

### Durable learning requires both initial success and later relearning

A single correct recitation is weak evidence of durable memory. Research on successive relearning combines repeated correct retrieval during an initial session with retrieval again across later sessions.

- [Rawson and Dunlosky, Optimizing Schedules of Retrieval Practice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21707204/): found strong retention from multiple correct initial recalls followed by spaced relearning sessions.
- [Rawson et al., The Power of Successive Relearning](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29431462/): evaluates successive relearning in an authentic course context.
- [Rawson and Dunlosky, Successive Relearning](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09637214221100484): an accessible review of the method, its evidence, and practical use.

**Implication for Lux Memory:** separate “I produced it correctly today” from “this has survived multiple delayed recalls.” A computed state can express that distinction without storing a permanent stage label.

### Spacing should respond to the desired retention period

There is no single scientifically correct sequence such as one day, seven days, and thirty days for every passage and learner. The useful gap depends partly on how long the material needs to be retained.

- [Cepeda et al., Spacing Effects in Learning](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/): found that the best study gap changed with the desired retention interval.
- [Karpicke and Roediger, Expanding Retrieval Practice Promotes Short-Term Retention, but Equally Spaced Retrieval Enhances Long-Term Retention](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576148/): cautions against assuming expanding intervals are always superior.
- [Settles and Meeder, A Trainable Spaced Repetition Model for Language Learning](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174/): describes Duolingo's Half-Life Regression model and how learner history can personalize predictions.
- [SuperMemo SM-2 algorithm](https://super-memory.org/archive/english/ol/sm2.htm): historically influential practical scheduling algorithm. It is useful background, not a prescription for Lux Memory.

**Implication for Lux Memory:** begin with a simple, inspectable scheduler, preserve the history needed to improve it, and treat every interval as a prediction that can be revised after the next recall.

### Successful effort is useful, but repeated failure is not the goal

More difficult retrieval can improve retention when the learner can still retrieve successfully. Difficulty should therefore rise by removing assistance gradually, not by trapping someone in repeated failure.

- [Pyc and Rawson, Testing the Retrieval Effort Hypothesis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749596X09000138): examines the relationship between retrieval difficulty and later memory.

**Implication for Lux Memory:** activities should form a fading-support ladder. Hints can appear after struggle, and assistance used should remain part of the attempt history.

### Speaking and melody may provide useful additional cues

Producing material aloud can improve memory compared with silent reading. Melody can also support verbatim recall under some conditions, although the design must avoid making performance or musical ability feel required.

- [MacLeod et al., The Production Effect](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20438265/): reports a memory benefit for words read aloud relative to silent reading.
- [Wallace, Memory for Music](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.20.6.1471): investigates conditions in which melody can aid text recall.

**Implication for Lux Memory:** spoken recall and optional musical repetition are promising activity modes. They should complement, not replace, a learner's ability to recall the passage without the recording.

## Scripture-memory field practice

These sources are valuable for understanding how Christians already memorize Scripture and the language they use around the discipline.

- [The Navigators, How to Memorize Scripture](https://downloads.navigators.org/resources/ddj/navigators_how_to_memorize_scripture.pdf): recommends understanding context, reading aloud, learning topic and reference, working phrase by phrase, reviewing, and applying the verse.
- [The Navigators, history of the Topical Memory System](https://history.navigators.org/the-topical-memory-system/): documents a long-running topic-based system that pairs references and verses.
- [Andrew Davis, An Approach to Extended Memorization of Scripture](https://scripturememory.com/downloadables/andrewdavis.pdf): describes memorizing whole books or extended passages through daily repetition and cumulative recitation.
- [Fighter Verses tools](https://www.fighterverses.com/tools): shows a ministry-developed system using weekly passages, songs, review, and community or family practice.
- [The Bible Memory App user manual](https://biblememory.com/pages/users-manual): documents a progression from typing first letters to mastery and scheduled review.

Ideas worth preserving from these practices include:

- Learn the reference with the text, not as detached metadata.
- Understand a passage in context before drilling its exact wording.
- Break long passages into meaningful phrases while still practicing the transitions between them.
- Recite cumulative spans so separate verses become one connected passage.
- Support topic collections without reducing Scripture to isolated proof texts.
- Make family, group, and spoken practice possible without requiring a social network.

## Product and user-experience references

These products help map the existing category. Their documentation and reviews should be treated as product signals, not controlled evidence.

- [Remember Me](https://www.remem.me/): combines multiple activity types, spaced review, audio, images, and collections.
- [Verses](https://www.getverses.com/): offers a polished, game-like memory flow and collection model.
- [Verses App Store listing and reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/verses-bible-memory/id939461663): useful for studying reactions to progression, translations, subscriptions, and platform support.
- [The Bible Memory App App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/the-bible-memory-app/id496790833?platform=iphone&see-all=reviews): useful for finding recurring praise and frustration around typing, review queues, sync, and reliability.
- [VerseLocker App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/bible-memory-verselocker/id1544933853?platform=iphone&see-all=reviews): useful for studying speech, audio, collections, and free-access expectations.
- [MemoryVerses App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/memoryverses-bible-memory/id6761134446): another recent interpretation of short daily Scripture-memory practice.
- [A user-built Scripture memory workflow discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/memorization/comments/1uet5j9/wanted_a_better_way_to_memorize_bible_verses_so_i/): an anecdotal example of demand for focused tools and custom workflows.

When reviewing competitors, record the underlying need rather than copying a feature. Examples include wanting to practice without a subscription, recovering after missing days, handling long passages, using a preferred translation, and trusting that sync will not lose years of work.

## Accounts, privacy, community, and support

These sources matter if optional accounts, accountability, shared plans, user-generated content, or financial support move beyond the exploratory stage in [roadmap.md](roadmap.md).

- [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/): review current rules for account deletion, user-generated content, privacy, payments, and person-to-person gifts before designing those features.
- [Google Play account deletion requirements](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13327111): describes deletion expectations for apps that allow account creation.
- [Google Play payments policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9858738): review before offering support payments inside the Android app.
- [European Commission, GDPR principles](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-business-and-organisations/principles-gdpr_en): summarizes data minimization, purpose limitation, storage limitation, and related principles.
- [European Commission, sensitive personal data](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-individuals_en): religious beliefs can be specially protected personal data. Scripture activity and group membership can reveal them even when the app never asks for a denomination.
- [FTC, Complying with COPPA](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions): relevant if social or account features are directed to children or knowingly collect their personal information.
- [IRS Publication 526, Charitable Contributions](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526): explains US charitable-contribution rules. Payments to a developer or ordinary business should not be represented as tax-deductible donations without an appropriate legal basis.

Early product implications:

- Keep the complete private memory practice usable without an account.
- Make sync and accountability explicit opt-in capabilities with clear explanations of what leaves the device.
- Store the minimum social data necessary and provide deletion from the beginning.
- Prefer constrained encouragement and progress sharing over open-ended feeds, comments, or public profiles.
- Describe financial support as a tip or contribution unless qualified legal advice and organizational status support the word “donation.”

## Important evidence gaps

The research does not yet answer several Lux-specific questions:

- Which practice sequence best supports exact, passage-length Scripture recall rather than word pairs or short prose?
- Should scheduling operate at the passage, verse, phrase, or difficult-transition level?
- How should an attempt with a small wording difference be scored across translations and editions?
- How much of a short session should overdue review be allowed to consume before the app protects learning, strengthening, or enjoyment?
- Does the garden metaphor encourage patient formation, or does it create guilt when a passage needs attention?
- Can speech recognition give fair, useful feedback across accents, environments, and Bible-specific names?
- Which accountability signals encourage practice without exposing sensitive religious behavior or producing shame?

Use [validation.md](validation.md) to turn these gaps into small, privacy-respecting product studies.
