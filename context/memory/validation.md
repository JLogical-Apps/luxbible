# Lux Memory Validation Guide

Status: exploratory

This file records the riskiest product assumptions that should be tested before Lux Memory treats them as settled behavior. It complements the evidence and gaps in [research.md](research.md).

## Principles

- Ask for explicit consent and explain what a study records.
- Collect the smallest amount of data that can answer the question.
- Keep ordinary app use private and free from behavioral analytics.
- Prefer delayed recall outcomes over completion, streak, or engagement metrics when evaluating learning.
- Include people memorizing single verses and people memorizing extended passages.
- Treat interviews and public reviews as signals, not proof.
- Avoid experiments that use shame, loss, or spiritual pressure to increase activity.

## Assumptions to validate

| Assumption | What to learn | A small first test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A fading-support activity ladder helps people reach unassisted recall | Which activities teach, which merely entertain, and where people feel stuck | Give learners the same short passage with two prototype activity sequences, then check unassisted recall after a delay |
| Dynamic replanning makes a time promise feel trustworthy | Whether a five-minute session really ends near five minutes and still feels spiritually worthwhile | Run repeated prototype sessions using estimated activity durations, record actual durations with consent, and interview learners about unfinished work |
| Review should have priority without consuming the whole session | Which session balance prevents backlog while preserving visible progress | Compare review-heavy and capped-review plans across several days, focusing on recall and perceived burden rather than raw activity count |
| Computed states are understandable | Whether labels such as Learning, Strengthening, Flourishing, and Needs attention match a learner's own sense of readiness | Show histories and proposed states without explaining the scheduler, then ask learners to predict what each state means and what should happen next |
| The garden metaphor supports formation without trivializing Scripture | Whether plants communicate patient care, or instead feel childish, manipulative, or guilt-producing | Test restrained list and progress views with and without plant imagery across different ages and aesthetic preferences |
| Passage units and transition practice help extended memorization | Where learners naturally split a passage and which seams cause failure | Observe people learning a paragraph or chapter excerpt, then compare phrase, verse, and transition-level error patterns |
| Spoken recall can be assessed fairly | Whether transcription errors can be distinguished from memory errors | Compare a learner's own judgment, a human-reviewed transcript, and automatic recognition across accents, quiet rooms, and ordinary background noise |
| Accountability can encourage without surveillance | What partners actually need to see and which information feels spiritually private | Prototype a few constrained signals, such as “practiced today” or a private encouragement, without exposing verse text, errors, or streak history by default |

## Questions for formative interviews

- What are you trying to memorize, and why?
- What do you currently do when first learning a passage?
- How do you know when you have memorized it?
- What usually causes a memorized passage to fade?
- What happens when you miss several days?
- Do you memorize exact wording, the thought, or both?
- How do references, context, audio, handwriting, music, and speaking affect your practice?
- Have existing apps ever made practice feel discouraging, artificial, or burdensome?
- What would you want an accountability partner to know? What should remain private?
- Would plants or a memory garden feel meaningful, childish, stressful, or neutral to you?

## Useful prototype sequence

1. Test passage selection and the first learning session with static prototypes.
2. Test the recall and feedback loop with a functional local prototype.
3. Test delayed recall across multiple days before tuning a long-term scheduler.
4. Test time-boxed dynamic replanning with both light and overloaded review queues.
5. Test the garden presentation as an optional visual layer over the same underlying states.
6. Test accountability only after private practice is useful on its own.

Record findings as dated notes and update the relevant product context only when the evidence changes a decision. Do not turn participant observations into permanent user profiles.
